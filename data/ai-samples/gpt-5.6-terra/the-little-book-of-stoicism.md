# The Little Book of Stoicism

## Ancient ideas for daily steadiness

Jonas Salzgeber’s *The Little Book of Stoicism* is an accessible introduction to Stoic philosophy as a practical discipline. Rather than presenting Stoicism as cold detachment, it describes a way of becoming calmer, more purposeful, and more resilient. The guiding distinction is familiar: some things are up to us—our judgments, choices, and efforts—while many things are not—other people, outcomes, the past, and mortality.

## Practice what is controllable

Salzgeber encourages readers to direct energy toward character rather than external approval. The four Stoic virtues—wisdom, courage, justice, and self-discipline—provide a compass when circumstances are messy. Wisdom asks what is true and useful; courage acts despite discomfort; justice remembers obligations to others; discipline keeps appetite and emotion from ruling the day.

The book includes exercises such as negative visualization, imagining the loss of something valued in order to appreciate it now; voluntary discomfort, choosing small hardships to reduce dependence on comfort; journaling; and reviewing a day’s actions. These practices are not about becoming grim. They are ways to reduce entitlement and make deliberate action more available under pressure.

Stoicism also has a social side. Human beings are part of a larger community, and virtue includes treating others fairly. A Stoic can resist wrongdoing, set boundaries, and pursue goals while recognizing that another person’s behavior is not fully controllable.

## What readers take away

*The Little Book of Stoicism* makes philosophy usable in ordinary moments: a delayed train, criticism at work, an argument at home, or anxiety about an uncertain outcome. Its enduring lesson is that peace comes less from arranging the world perfectly than from training one’s response to an imperfect world.
