# Rapport

## Connection without manipulation

In *Rapport*, Emily and Laurence Alison draw on their experience in forensic psychology, policing, and high-stakes interviewing to explain how trust can be built with people who are guarded, angry, frightened, or resistant. Their version of rapport is not charm or a bag of tricks. It is the capacity to make another person feel sufficiently safe, respected, and understood that communication becomes possible.

The authors emphasize that people often mistake agreement for rapport. Real rapport does not require approving of someone’s beliefs or behavior. It requires taking their perspective seriously enough to understand what they are trying to protect, achieve, or express. This distinction is vital in conflict, leadership, parenting, and negotiation, where forced friendliness can feel false.

## The four skills

Their framework highlights four interpersonal moves: autonomy, reflection, empathy, and language. Autonomy means recognizing that people need a sense of choice. Orders and pressure can trigger resistance, while honest options invite cooperation. Reflection involves accurately feeding back what a person has said, especially the meaning and emotion behind their words. Empathy is the effort to understand another person’s experience without losing one’s own judgment. Language concerns tone, pace, pronouns, and questions—the small signals that communicate respect or threat.

The Alisons also describe common unhelpful styles. Some people over-control, trying to force an outcome. Others over-accommodate, avoiding necessary challenge. Some detach into cold logic, while others become emotionally flooded. Effective rapport requires flexibility: enough warmth to create safety and enough clarity to keep a conversation grounded.

## What readers take away

*Rapport* gives readers a more serious view of listening. Instead of asking how to win someone over, it asks what conditions allow both people to think clearly together. In practice, that means slowing down, asking genuine questions, naming concerns accurately, and allowing another person dignity even during disagreement. The result is not guaranteed agreement, but it is a far better starting point for change.
