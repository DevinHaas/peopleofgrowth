# Never Split the Difference

## Negotiation as human connection

Chris Voss, with Tahl Raz, brings lessons from FBI hostage negotiation to everyday bargaining in *Never Split the Difference*. Its provocative title challenges the idea that compromise is always wise. Splitting a difference can produce an arbitrary result, especially when the parties have not understood what the other actually needs. Voss’s alternative is tactical empathy: showing another person that their perspective has been heard while still pursuing a clear objective.

## Listen for the real problem

The book’s core skills are designed to reduce defensiveness. Mirroring repeats the last few important words as a question, encouraging the other person to elaborate. Labeling puts emotions into words: “It seems like you’re worried about…” A carefully delivered “That’s right” signals that someone feels understood; it is more meaningful than a reluctant “you’re right.” Open-ended calibrated questions—often beginning with how or what—invite the other side to solve a problem without feeling ordered around.

Voss also values a productive “no.” People often feel safer when they can reject a proposal than when they are pressed to agree. Questions such as “Would it be ridiculous to…?” can give the other party autonomy. The aim is not manipulation for its own sake, but information. A negotiator needs to discover fears, constraints, status concerns, and unspoken alternatives before proposing terms.

## Prepare, then adapt

The authors recommend an accusation audit: name the negative things the other party may be thinking before they use them as weapons. They also encourage identifying “black swans,” the hidden pieces of information that change the whole negotiation. Preparation includes knowing one’s goals, alternatives, and boundaries, but Voss repeatedly warns against becoming so attached to a script that one stops listening.

## What readers take away

*Never Split the Difference* is most useful when read as a book about difficult conversations, not just price haggling. Its methods encourage calm curiosity under pressure. Readers learn to slow the exchange down, acknowledge emotion, ask questions that uncover reality, and refuse false urgency. The strongest lesson is that people negotiate better when they feel understood—and when they understand themselves what they will and will not trade away.
