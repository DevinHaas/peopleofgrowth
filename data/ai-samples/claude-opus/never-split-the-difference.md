# Never Split the Difference by Chris Voss and Tahl Raz

Chris Voss spent more than two decades at the FBI, much of it as the bureau's lead international kidnapping negotiator. He negotiated with bank robbers, terrorists, and kidnappers in situations where a mistake could cost someone their life. In *Never Split the Difference*, written with journalist Tahl Raz, he argues that the same techniques that work in a hostage crisis work in everyday life: asking for a raise, buying a car, negotiating with a landlord, or even getting your kids to bed.

The book's title is a provocation. Most negotiation advice tells you to find a compromise, to meet in the middle. Voss argues that compromise is often a bad outcome. If your spouse wants you to wear black shoes and you want to wear brown, wearing one of each is the compromise, and nobody is happy. Good negotiation isn't about splitting the difference. It's about understanding the other side so deeply that you can find a better solution.

## Why Traditional Negotiation Falls Short

Voss opens by describing his early encounters with academic negotiation theory, particularly the rational, problem-solving approach associated with Harvard's *Getting to Yes*. That model assumes people are logical actors who will respond to reasonable arguments and objective criteria.

The trouble, Voss argues, is that humans aren't purely rational. Drawing on the work of psychologists like Daniel Kahneman, he points out that we're driven by emotion, bias, and fear far more than we like to admit. A negotiation strategy that ignores emotion will fail when the stakes are high. The FBI's approach, which Voss helped refine, is built on empathy, not to be nice, but because understanding the other person's emotions is the key to influencing them.

## Be a Mirror

Voss's core philosophy is that negotiation is primarily about information gathering. The goal is to discover what the other side actually wants, what they fear, and what constraints they're under. To do that, you need them talking.

### Active listening

The first skill is genuinely listening. Most people in a negotiation are so busy thinking about their own arguments that they barely hear the other side. Voss recommends slowing down, focusing entirely on the other person, and making them feel safe.

### The late-night FM DJ voice

Tone matters enormously. Voss describes three voices: the assertive voice, which is usually counterproductive; the positive, playful voice, which should be your default; and the late-night FM DJ voice, a calm, slow, downward-inflecting tone that signals control and reassurance. Using the right voice can calm a tense situation before you've even said anything substantive.

### Mirroring

Mirroring is one of the simplest and most effective techniques in the book. You repeat the last one to three words the other person said, usually with an upward inflection, as a question. For example, if someone says, "We can't do that because of the budget," you say, "Because of the budget?" This encourages them to elaborate, often revealing more information, and it builds rapport because people feel heard.

Voss tells a story of a colleague who used mirroring with her boss to push back on an unreasonable request without ever directly refusing. By simply mirroring and staying quiet, she got him to explain his reasoning and eventually reconsider.

## Don't Feel Their Pain, Label It

Tactical empathy is the heart of the book. It means understanding the feelings and mindset of the other person and acknowledging them out loud. It doesn't mean agreeing with them.

The tool for this is labeling. You name the emotion you think the other person is feeling, using phrases like "It seems like…," "It sounds like…," or "It looks like…" For example: "It sounds like you're frustrated with how long this has taken."

Labeling has a surprising effect. Naming a negative emotion tends to diffuse it, while naming a positive emotion reinforces it. And because you're using neutral language ("it seems like" rather than "I think you"), the other person doesn't feel accused.

Voss also recommends doing an "accusation audit" at the start of difficult conversations. List all the negative things the other side might be thinking about you and say them first. "You're probably going to think I'm being unreasonable…" When you name the accusations before they do, they lose their sting, and the other person often responds by reassuring you.

## Beware "Yes," Master "No"

Many salespeople are trained to get a series of small yeses to build momentum toward a big one. Voss thinks this is a mistake. People know when they're being led, and they say "yes" defensively just to get you off their back.

Voss distinguishes three kinds of yes: the counterfeit yes (said to escape), the confirmation yes (a simple affirmation), and the commitment yes (a real agreement that leads to action). Only the last one matters.

Instead of chasing yes, Voss encourages you to invite "no." Saying no makes people feel safe and in control. It's a way of protecting themselves, and once they feel protected, they're more willing to engage. You might ask, "Is now a bad time to talk?" rather than "Do you have a few minutes?" The "no" answer gives them a sense of autonomy while actually opening the conversation.

## Trigger the Two Words That Transform Any Negotiation

The two words Voss wants to hear are "That's right." When someone says "that's right," it means they feel genuinely understood. You've accurately captured their worldview.

To get there, you use a summary: a combination of paraphrasing what they've said and labeling the emotions behind it. When you get it right, the other person's resistance often drops dramatically.

Importantly, "You're right" is not the same thing. People say "you're right" when they want you to stop talking. "That's right" signals a real breakthrough.

## Bend Their Reality

This chapter is about how to influence the other side's perception of the deal. Voss explains that people are more motivated by avoiding loss than by achieving gain, a principle known as loss aversion. Framing your offer in terms of what they'll lose if they don't accept can be more powerful than emphasizing what they'll gain.

He also discusses anchoring, the idea that the first number mentioned shapes the rest of the negotiation. Rather than throwing out a number first, he often suggests letting the other side anchor, or using an extreme anchor to shift expectations. He also suggests using odd, specific numbers, like $37,263 rather than $37,000, because they seem more carefully calculated and less arbitrary.

Another tip: deadlines are often artificial, and people tend to make rushed concessions as they approach. Being aware of this, and not being bound by your own deadlines, gives you an advantage.

## Create the Illusion of Control

The calibrated question is one of the most powerful tools in the book. These are open-ended questions, usually starting with "how" or "what," that invite the other side to solve your problem for you.

For example: "How am I supposed to do that?" This simple question forces the other person to consider your constraints and come up with a solution. It's a polite way of saying no without saying no.

Other calibrated questions include:

- "What about this is important to you?"
- "How can I help make this better for us?"
- "What's the biggest challenge you face?"
- "How would you like me to proceed?"

These questions give the other person a sense of control while subtly steering the conversation in your direction. Voss avoids "why" questions because they tend to make people defensive.

## Guarantee Execution

Getting agreement isn't the same as getting results. Voss warns that a deal isn't done until it's implemented. You need to make sure the people who will actually carry out the deal are on board, including those who aren't at the table.

He introduces the "rule of three," which means getting the other side to agree to the same thing three times in different ways. This confirms their commitment is real. He also suggests paying attention to body language and tone, since words often contradict what people actually feel. And he notes the "Pinocchio effect": people who are lying often use more words and more third-person pronouns than those telling the truth.

## Bargain Hard

For price negotiations, Voss offers the Ackerman model, a structured system he learned at the FBI:

1. Set your target price.
2. Make your first offer at 65 percent of the target.
3. Raise in decreasing increments to 85, 95, and finally 100 percent.
4. Use empathy and different ways of saying no to get the other side to counter before you raise your offer.
5. When calculating the final amount, use precise, non-round numbers.
6. On your final number, throw in a non-monetary item to show you're at your limit.

The decreasing increments signal that you're reaching your limit, and the precise final number suggests careful calculation.

He also discusses different negotiator types: analysts, who are methodical and need time; accommodators, who value relationships and want to be friendly; and assertives, who prioritize being heard and getting things done. Understanding your own type and the other person's helps you adjust your approach.

## Find the Black Swan

The final chapter introduces "black swans," unknown pieces of information that, once discovered, completely change the negotiation. Every negotiation has them. They might be a hidden constraint, a personal fear, or an unspoken motivation.

You find black swans by listening closely, asking questions, and paying attention to things that don't seem to fit. Voss shares stories where uncovering one small detail turned a stalemate into a resolution. He also emphasizes that when the other side seems irrational, they usually aren't; they're likely operating with information you don't have, constraints you don't know about, or interests you haven't uncovered.

## Key Takeaways

**Negotiation is about emotion, not just logic.** People are driven by feelings, and understanding those feelings is the key to influence.

**Listen more than you talk.** Information is the currency of negotiation, and you get it by listening.

**Use mirrors and labels.** These simple techniques build rapport and get people talking.

**Aim for "that's right," not "yes."** Real agreement comes when the other side feels genuinely understood.

**Ask calibrated questions.** "How" and "what" questions give the other side a sense of control while steering the conversation.

**Don't compromise by default.** Splitting the difference often leaves everyone unsatisfied.

**Look for the black swan.** The information that will transform the negotiation is usually hidden.

## What I Took Away

*Never Split the Difference* is one of the most practical books I've read on communication. The stories from Voss's FBI career make it gripping, and the techniques are specific enough to try the same day.

What surprised me most was how much of the book is really about empathy. I went in expecting hardball tactics and came out thinking about how rarely I truly listen. Mirroring and labeling feel awkward at first, but they work, and not just in formal negotiations. They make conversations with friends, family, and coworkers better because people feel heard.

The calibrated question "How am I supposed to do that?" has become one of my favorite tools. It turns a confrontation into a collaboration and gently puts the problem back on the table for both sides to solve.

Some readers find the book a bit too pleased with itself, and a few techniques can feel manipulative if used carelessly. But at its core, the message is respectful: understand people deeply, take their emotions seriously, and you'll get better outcomes for everyone. If you want to become better at any kind of conversation where something is at stake, this book is a must-read.
