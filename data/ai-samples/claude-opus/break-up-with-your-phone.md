# Break Up with Your Phone by Catherine Price

Most of us have a relationship with our phones that we would never accept from a person. We check it first thing in the morning and last thing at night. We reach for it in line at the grocery store, at red lights, in the middle of conversations. We feel a small jolt of panic when we can't find it. And yet, if someone asked whether we were happy with how much time we spend on it, most of us would say no.

Catherine Price's *Break Up with Your Phone* starts from that uncomfortable gap. It is a short, practical book with a simple premise: you don't have to throw your phone in a lake, but you do need to renegotiate the terms of the relationship. The goal isn't abstinence. It's a healthier, more intentional connection where you use the phone instead of the phone using you.

## Part One: The Wake-Up Call

The first half of the book explains why breaking up is so hard. Price walks through the design choices that make smartphones and apps so sticky, and the picture isn't flattering to the tech industry. Many of the most popular apps are built by teams whose job is to maximize "engagement," which is a polite word for capturing as much of your attention as possible. Your attention is the product being sold to advertisers.

To do that, apps borrow tricks from slot machines. Pull-to-refresh is a lever. Notifications arrive on an unpredictable schedule, which is the most habit-forming reward pattern there is. Infinite scroll removes natural stopping points. Red badges create a low-grade itch that you can only scratch by opening the app. None of this is accidental.

Price ties this to dopamine, the brain chemical associated with anticipation and seeking. Every time we check our phone, there is a small chance of something new and rewarding, and that chance is enough to keep us checking. Over time, the checking becomes automatic. We pick up the phone without deciding to.

She also looks at what all this checking costs us. Constant switching between tasks fragments attention and makes deep focus harder. Our ability to remember things suffers when we are always half-distracted. Sleep gets worse when screens follow us to bed. Relationships thin out when the people in front of us compete with the people in our pockets. Perhaps most importantly, we lose the empty moments where creativity and reflection used to happen. Boredom, it turns out, was doing useful work.

Her point is not that phones are evil. It's that they were designed with goals that are often different from ours, and if we don't set our own terms, theirs win by default.

## Part Two: The Breakup

The second half is a 30-day plan, and this is where the book earns its place on a shelf. Rather than asking you to quit cold turkey, Price walks you through a gradual process of awareness, change, and reconnection.

### Week one: notice

The first steps are about paying attention. Install a tracking app to see how much time you actually spend on your phone and how many times you pick it up. Most people are shocked by the numbers. Then start noticing how you feel before and after you check. Are you bored, anxious, avoiding something? Do you feel better afterward, or slightly worse? This simple observation starts to break the automatic loop.

### Week two: change your habits

Next you start making practical changes. Delete the apps that drain you the most, especially social media, and use them through a browser if you must. Turn off nearly all notifications, keeping only the ones from actual humans you want to hear from. Rearrange your home screen so only useful tools are visible and the tempting stuff is buried in folders. Stop sleeping with your phone next to your bed; buy an old-fashioned alarm clock instead.

Price also suggests creating physical speed bumps. A rubber band around the phone, or a lock screen that asks "What for? Why now? What else?", gives you a moment to decide whether you really want to pick it up. That pause is the whole game.

She also recommends setting up "no-phone zones," such as the dinner table or the bedroom, and practicing being out in the world without your phone for short periods to remind yourself that nothing bad happens.

### Weeks three and four: reclaim your life

The later steps are about filling the space you've opened up. Price encourages a "trial separation," a 24-hour period completely away from your phone. Many readers dread it, and many find it unexpectedly peaceful. She asks you to think about what you actually want to do with your time and attention: hobbies you've neglected, people you want to see, books you meant to read. The point of the breakup is not to stare at a wall. It's to make room for things that matter more.

The plan ends with checking in on your new relationship and making adjustments. This isn't a one-time fix but an ongoing practice.

## Key Ideas Worth Remembering

**Your attention is valuable.** What you pay attention to becomes your life. Companies understand this and are paying billions to capture it. You should value it at least as highly as they do.

**Awareness comes before change.** You can't fix a habit you don't see. Simply noticing when and why you reach for your phone is surprisingly powerful.

**Design your environment.** Willpower is unreliable. It's much easier to change your settings, delete apps, and keep the phone out of the bedroom than to resist temptation over and over again.

**Pause before you pick up.** A tiny moment of asking "what am I doing this for?" turns an automatic behavior into a choice.

**Replace, don't just remove.** If you only take something away, the void will pull you back. Decide what you want to do with your reclaimed time.

## What I Took Away

What I appreciate about this book is its tone. Price isn't preachy, and she admits she struggled with the same problems she's writing about. The book reads like advice from a smart friend who has done the research and wants you to feel better, not guilty.

The most useful lesson for me was the reframe from "screen time" to "attention." Counting minutes can feel arbitrary, but asking whether the way I spend my attention matches what I care about is a much sharper question. A long phone call with a friend is screen time too, and it's time well spent. Mindless scrolling at midnight is not.

If you've ever looked up from your phone and wondered where the last hour went, *Break Up with Your Phone* is a quick, practical read that can genuinely change your daily habits. You probably won't get rid of your phone. But you might start to feel like the one in charge again.
