# Meditations by Marcus Aurelius (translated by Gregory Hays)

Imagine finding the private journal of the most powerful person in the world. Not a public speech or a carefully crafted memoir, but notes written to himself late at night, reminding himself how to be a better person. That's what *Meditations* is.

Marcus Aurelius was Roman emperor from 161 to 180 AD. He ruled during a period of war, plague, and political betrayal. Much of *Meditations* was written while he was on military campaign along the Danube frontier. He never intended it to be published. The original Greek title translates roughly to "To Himself."

Gregory Hays's translation, published in 2002, is widely loved because it reads like modern English. Older translations can feel stiff and archaic. Hays makes Marcus sound like a thoughtful, slightly weary man talking to himself, which is exactly what he was. His introduction is also an excellent short guide to Marcus's life and to Stoic philosophy.

## What Kind of Book Is It?

*Meditations* isn't organized like a typical book. It's divided into twelve "books," but these are really collections of short entries, some just a sentence long, others a page or two. There's no argument building from start to finish. Marcus repeats himself often, returning to the same few ideas again and again. That repetition is the point. He was using writing as a form of practice, drilling the principles into himself.

The first book is different from the others. It's a list of people Marcus is grateful to and what he learned from each of them: his grandfather, his mother, his teachers, and especially his adoptive father, the emperor Antoninus Pius. It's a beautiful opening, a reminder that character is shaped by the people around us.

## The Core Ideas

### Control what you can, accept what you can't

At the heart of Stoicism is the distinction between things within our control and things outside it. Our judgments, intentions, and actions are ours. Everything else, including our bodies, our reputation, other people, and events, is not.

Marcus returns to this constantly. Disturbance, he says, doesn't come from things themselves but from our judgments about them. If something upsets you, it's your interpretation that's doing the damage, and you can change that. "Choose not to be harmed—and you won't feel harmed. Don't feel harmed—and you haven't been."

### The obstacle is the way

One of the most quoted passages captures how Marcus approached adversity. The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way. Rather than seeing problems as interruptions, a Stoic sees them as material to work with, an opportunity to practice patience, courage, or justice.

### Other people

Marcus spent his days surrounded by flatterers, rivals, and difficult people. A famous entry starts with him preparing for the day: when you wake up, tell yourself that you'll meet people who are meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and surly. They are like this because they can't tell good from evil. But he goes on to say that they are his kin, and that no one can implicate him in ugliness. We were made to work together, like hands and feet.

This is one of the most humane parts of the book. Marcus wasn't naive about people's flaws, but he refused to let them make him bitter. He reminded himself repeatedly that people who do wrong do so out of ignorance, and that the best revenge is not to be like them.

### Duty and the common good

Marcus believed that human beings are social creatures, made to help one another. He saw himself as part of a larger whole, and he often reminds himself to do his job, to act for the good of others, and to stop arguing about what a good person is and simply be one.

There's a lovely passage about getting out of bed. Marcus admits he'd prefer to stay under the blankets. But he asks himself whether he was born to feel nice or to do the work of a human being. Even emperors struggled with mornings.

### Impermanence and death

Death is everywhere in *Meditations*, not in a morbid way but as a clarifying reminder. Marcus constantly reflects on how short life is and how quickly everyone is forgotten. Alexander the Great and his mule driver both died and ended up in the same place. Fame is meaningless because the people who remember you will soon be gone too.

This awareness isn't meant to depress. It's meant to focus. If life is short, we shouldn't waste it on trivial worries, grudges, or vanity. Do each thing as if it were the last thing you do in life.

### The present moment

Closely tied to impermanence is the idea that we only ever live in the present. The past is gone and the future is uncertain. Marcus reminds himself that no one can lose any life but the one they are living now. Concentrate on what's in front of you and do it well.

### The view from above

Marcus often imagines looking down on the world from a great height, seeing cities, armies, weddings, funerals, all the busy activity of humanity, and realizing how small it all is. This perspective helps shrink his own troubles to their proper size.

## Why It Still Resonates

What makes *Meditations* remarkable is the person writing it. Marcus had almost unlimited power. He could have indulged every desire. Instead, he spent his evenings reminding himself to be humble, patient, and fair. He was not perfect, and he knew it. The book is full of self-correction, as if he were constantly falling short and trying again.

That honesty is part of why the book feels so modern. It's not written by a sage on a mountain. It's written by a tired man with a hard job trying to stay decent.

## What I Took Away

I come back to *Meditations* more than almost any other book. It's not something you read once. It's something you keep on your nightstand and open at random. A single entry can reset a bad day.

The idea I find most useful is that our distress comes from our judgments, not events. It's a simple reframe, but practiced consistently, it changes how you handle almost everything, from traffic to criticism to genuine loss.

I also love the morning passage about difficult people. It's a realistic and compassionate way to start the day: expect friction, don't be surprised by it, and don't let it change who you are.

If you're new to Stoicism, the Hays translation is the place to start. It's clear, warm, and surprisingly personal. Nearly two thousand years later, Marcus is still one of the best teachers on how to live.
