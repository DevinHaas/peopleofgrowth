# The Little Book of Stoicism by Jonas Salzgeber

Stoicism has become one of the most popular philosophies of the modern self-improvement world. Entrepreneurs quote Marcus Aurelius, athletes talk about controlling what they can control, and podcasts break down the ideas of Seneca and Epictetus for a new generation. But diving into the original texts can be intimidating. They're old, sometimes repetitive, and full of references that require context.

Jonas Salzgeber's *The Little Book of Stoicism* is designed to bridge that gap. It's a friendly, practical introduction that explains what Stoicism is, where it came from, and, most importantly, how to use it in everyday life. Salzgeber, who runs the website NJlifehacks, writes in a warm, conversational style and fills the book with simple exercises.

## Part One: What Is Stoicism?

Salzgeber begins with a brief history. Stoicism was founded in Athens around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, a merchant who lost everything in a shipwreck and turned to philosophy. The name comes from the *Stoa Poikile*, the painted porch where Zeno taught. Over the following centuries, the philosophy spread to Rome, where its three most famous figures lived: Seneca, a statesman and playwright; Epictetus, a former slave who became a teacher; and Marcus Aurelius, the emperor.

Salzgeber emphasizes that Stoicism was always meant to be practical. It wasn't an abstract exercise in debate. It was a way of life, a set of tools for living well regardless of circumstances.

### The Stoic Happiness Triangle

To make the core ideas easy to remember, Salzgeber presents what he calls the Stoic Happiness Triangle. It has three corners, with a fourth idea at the center.

**Live with areté.** Areté is often translated as "virtue," but Salzgeber explains it as expressing your highest self, being the best version of yourself in every moment. The Stoics identified four cardinal virtues: wisdom, justice, courage, and self-discipline (or temperance). Living with areté means acting in line with these virtues.

**Focus on what you control.** This is the most famous Stoic principle. Some things are up to us, such as our judgments, choices, and actions. Most things are not, including other people, the weather, the economy, and even our own bodies to a degree. The Stoics teach that we should focus our energy on what we control and accept the rest.

**Take responsibility.** Since events themselves are outside our control but our responses are not, we're responsible for how we handle whatever happens. It's not things that upset us, as Epictetus said, but our judgments about things. This means we can't blame external circumstances for our unhappiness.

At the center of the triangle is **eudaimonia**, the ultimate goal of Stoic life. It's often translated as happiness, but it's closer to flourishing or living a good life. The Stoics believed that eudaimonia comes from living with virtue, not from external success.

### The Stoic Archer

Salzgeber uses a helpful metaphor to illustrate the focus on what we control: the archer. An archer can control their training, their preparation, their aim, and the moment they release the arrow. But once the arrow is in the air, a gust of wind might push it off course. The archer can't control that. The Stoic archer focuses on doing their best and accepts the outcome with calm, whatever it is.

This metaphor captures a key Stoic attitude: care deeply about your effort, but hold the outcome lightly.

### Preferred indifferents

A common misconception is that Stoics don't care about anything external. Salzgeber explains the concept of "preferred indifferents." Things like health, wealth, and good relationships aren't necessary for a good life, according to the Stoics, but they're naturally preferable. It's fine to pursue them, as long as you don't make your happiness depend on them and you never sacrifice virtue to get them.

### Bad feelings and the Stoic mindset

The book also addresses the misconception that Stoics suppress emotions. Salzgeber clarifies that the Stoics weren't against feelings; they were against being ruled by destructive emotions such as anger, fear, and excessive desire. They believed that these emotions often arise from mistaken judgments, and that by examining and correcting those judgments, we can reduce their power over us.

## Part Two: 55 Stoic Practices

The second and larger part of the book is a collection of 55 practical exercises. This is where the book really shines. Rather than just describing the philosophy, Salzgeber gives you things to do. Here are some of the most memorable.

### Negative visualization

The Stoics practiced *premeditatio malorum*, imagining things going wrong. This might sound pessimistic, but it serves several purposes. It prepares you for setbacks, reduces the shock if they happen, and increases gratitude for what you have right now. Imagining losing a loved one, for example, can make you appreciate them more today.

### The morning and evening routines

Salzgeber recommends starting each day with a reflection on what challenges you might face and how you want to respond, much like Marcus Aurelius did. In the evening, review your day. What did you do well? Where did you fall short? What can you improve? This habit, inspired by Seneca, builds self-awareness over time.

### Voluntary discomfort

Occasionally choosing discomfort, such as taking a cold shower, fasting, or sleeping on the floor, builds resilience and reminds you that you can handle hardship. It also helps you appreciate comforts you might take for granted.

### The view from above

Imagine zooming out and seeing your situation from a great height, then the city, then the country, then the planet. This perspective helps put your problems in context and reminds you how small many of your worries are.

### Memento mori

Remember that you will die. The Stoics used this reminder not to create fear, but to sharpen focus. Life is short, so don't waste it on trivial things.

### Amor fati

Love your fate. Rather than merely accepting what happens, embrace it. Treat everything that happens as material for growth.

### Don't take it personally

When someone insults or wrongs you, remember that their behavior comes from their own judgments and limitations. You can choose not to be harmed by it.

### Find a role model

The Stoics recommended choosing someone admirable and asking what they would do in your situation. This helps clarify the right action in difficult moments.

### Be a lifelong learner

Stoicism is a practice, not a destination. Keep reading, reflecting, and learning.

### Other exercises

Other practices include examining your impressions before reacting, practicing self-discipline in small ways, being kind to others even when they're difficult, focusing on the present moment, using the reserve clause ("if nothing prevents it") when making plans, and treating setbacks as training.

## Key Takeaways

**Stoicism is practical.** It's meant to be lived, not just studied.

**Focus on what you control.** Your judgments and actions are yours. Most else isn't.

**Virtue is the path to a good life.** Being your best self matters more than external success.

**Emotions come from judgments.** Change how you interpret events, and you change how you feel about them.

**Practice regularly.** Exercises like negative visualization and evening reflection turn philosophy into habit.

## What I Took Away

*The Little Book of Stoicism* is an excellent introduction for anyone curious about Stoicism but unsure where to start. Salzgeber makes the ideas clear and accessible without dumbing them down, and his enthusiasm is infectious.

The Stoic archer metaphor has become one of my favorite ways to think about effort and outcomes. I can't control whether a project succeeds, but I can control how well I prepare and how hard I work. Focusing on that relieves a lot of anxiety.

The 55 practices are the real value of the book. You don't need to try all of them. Pick a few that resonate and make them part of your routine. For me, the evening review and negative visualization have been the most helpful.

Some readers who already know Stoicism well may find the book a bit basic, and the tone is more self-help than scholarly. But that's exactly its purpose. If you want a practical, encouraging guide to living a calmer and more meaningful life, this is a great place to begin. And it may well lead you to the original works of Seneca, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius.
