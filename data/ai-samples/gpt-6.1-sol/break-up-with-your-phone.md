# Break Up with Your Phone by Catherine Price

Most of us do not decide to spend an evening looking at our phones. We check one notification, follow another, and eventually discover that the evening has disappeared. Catherine Price's *Break Up with Your Phone* starts with this familiar gap between intention and behavior. The problem is not simply that phones take time. It is that they can quietly decide what receives our attention, even when we would rather be doing something else.

Despite its title, the book does not ask everyone to abandon smartphones. Price wants readers to end an unhealthy relationship and build a more deliberate one. A phone can remain useful without becoming the default answer to boredom, uncertainty, loneliness, or any spare moment. The aim is not technological purity. It is recovering the ability to choose.

## Understand the relationship before changing it

The first part of the book explains why stepping away can feel surprisingly difficult. Many apps offer a steady stream of novelty, social feedback, and unpredictable rewards. We never know whether the next check will deliver something interesting, reassuring, or urgent. That uncertainty helps make checking feel worthwhile even when most checks accomplish very little.

Price connects these design features with ordinary human needs. Wanting connection, stimulation, and recognition is not a character flaw. But a product can exploit those needs without satisfying them very well. Scrolling through other people's updates may briefly relieve loneliness while leaving us less available to the people sitting beside us.

This changes the emotional tone of the problem. Instead of asking, “Why am I so weak?” we can ask, “What is prompting this behavior, and what am I actually looking for?” That question creates room for practical changes rather than another round of self-criticism.

Price also challenges the idea that every glance is harmless because it lasts only a few seconds. Attention does not always return immediately to its previous task. Repeated interruptions can make reading, conversation, and sustained work feel increasingly uncomfortable. The important issue is not whether a phone has permanently damaged a brain. It is whether our daily habits are training the kind of attention we want to have.

## Notice what your phone gives you and takes away

Before introducing restrictions, Price asks readers to observe their existing behavior. When do you reach for the phone? What are you doing on it? How do you feel before and afterward? Usage tracking can help, but numbers are only part of the picture.

An hour spent speaking to a distant friend is different from an hour spent refreshing a feed that leaves you anxious. Even the same app can serve different purposes at different times. A useful relationship with technology depends on distinguishing meaningful use from automatic use, not treating every minute on a screen as equally bad.

The book encourages readers to identify what they want more of: uninterrupted meals, better sleep, long-form reading, creative work, time outdoors, or simply the ability to be alone without constant stimulation. This is an important shift. Removing a habit creates a gap. Knowing what belongs in that gap makes change more attractive and more sustainable.

One helpful practice is to pause before unlocking the screen and ask what you intend to do. You might discover that you genuinely need directions. You might also discover that you have no purpose beyond escaping a slightly uncomfortable feeling. Either answer gives you information. The pause interrupts the assumption that an urge must become an action.

## Change the environment, not just your intentions

The second part of the book offers a thirty-day plan. It moves from observation toward practical experiments and a more lasting set of boundaries. The structure matters because a healthier relationship is easier to build through specific actions than through a sweeping promise to “use my phone less.”

Price recommends reducing unnecessary notifications, removing especially tempting apps, and rearranging the home screen so useful tools are easier to access than compulsive distractions. These changes introduce friction where automatic habits previously ran without resistance.

Physical boundaries are equally valuable. Charging the phone outside the bedroom can protect the beginning and end of the day. Keeping it off the table during meals makes conversation less vulnerable to interruption. Choosing a regular place to leave it at home prevents every room from becoming another checking station.

These suggestions are not interchangeable commandments. Someone whose job requires availability may need different boundaries from someone who checks mainly out of habit. The principle is to make the desired behavior easier and the unwanted behavior less automatic.

A particularly revealing exercise is a twenty-four-hour separation from the phone. The point is not to prove toughness. It is to notice what surfaces when the usual distraction is unavailable. There may be boredom, anxiety, relief, or a surprising amount of free time. Preparing alternatives in advance helps make the experiment informative rather than merely frustrating.

## Rebuild attention through ordinary life

Putting down a phone does not automatically restore concentration. Price encourages activities that let attention settle: reading without switching tasks, taking a walk without constant input, listening closely to another person, and allowing an idle moment to remain idle.

Mindfulness is useful here because it teaches us to notice an impulse without immediately obeying it. You can feel the urge to check and still stay with the page, the conversation, or the uncomfortable emotion. Repeating that small choice is part of rebuilding agency.

The book also leaves room for adjustment. A stressful week or a new app can bring old habits back. That does not make the experiment a failure. It means the relationship needs maintenance. Regularly reviewing what is working is more realistic than expecting one digital cleanup to solve the problem forever.

## What to take away

The strongest lesson in *Break Up with Your Phone* is that attention is part of a life, not merely a productivity resource. What repeatedly captures it helps shape our relationships, memories, and experience of time.

Price offers a practical way to make those choices more conscious. Start by observing without judgment. Decide what deserves more space. Change the environment, test clear boundaries, and practice tolerating the urge to check. A successful breakup does not leave you without technology. It leaves you with a phone that supports your life rather than continually interrupting it.
