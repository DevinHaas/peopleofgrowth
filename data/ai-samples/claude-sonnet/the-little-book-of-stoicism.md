Stoicism is an ancient Greek and Roman philosophy that has found a surprising new audience among entrepreneurs, athletes, and anyone trying to stay steady in a noisy world. Jonas Salzgeber's *The Little Book of Stoicism* is one of the most approachable introductions to the subject. Rather than diving deep into academic history, it takes the core ideas of Stoicism and turns them into 25 practical tools that readers can use every day.

## What the book is about

Salzgeber is a Swiss writer who discovered Stoicism while struggling with stress and worry, and he began writing about it online to share what helped. His goal in the book is modest and clear: to make Stoic wisdom useful. He draws primarily on the works of three Roman thinkers, Seneca, Epictetus, and Marcus Aurelius, and on earlier Greek ideas, then translates them into plain modern language with examples.

The book begins with a short overview of what Stoicism is. At its heart, he explains, it is a philosophy of life aimed at living well and finding tranquility, not by avoiding hardship but by changing how we respond to it. The Stoics believed that virtue, which includes wisdom, courage, justice, and self-discipline, is the highest good and the key to a fulfilling life. External things such as wealth, status, and even health are "preferred indifferents": pleasant to have, but not essential to happiness.

## The core principles

Before the tools, Salzgeber lays out a handful of principles that underpin them.

**The dichotomy of control.** Some things are up to us, like our opinions, choices, and actions, while others are not, like the weather, other people's behavior, and the past. Peace of mind depends on focusing energy on the first group and accepting the second. This is probably the most quoted Stoic idea, and it reappears throughout the book.

**Perception shapes experience.** It is not events themselves that upset us but our judgments about them. By examining and adjusting our interpretations, we can reduce suffering.

**Live according to virtue.** Ethical behavior is not an extra but the point. A good person acts with integrity and treats others fairly.

**Remember mortality.** Awareness of death helps us prioritize and appreciate our limited time.

## The 25 tools

The main body of the book consists of the 25 tools, each described in a few pages with an explanation, a quote from a Stoic, and a practical suggestion. They are grouped by theme but can be read in any order. A few highlights illustrate the range.

**Negative visualization.** Imagine losing the things you value, such as your job, your health, or your loved ones. This is not meant to be gloomy. It helps you appreciate what you have and prepares you for setbacks so they hit less hard.

**Pause and reflect.** When something upsets you, stop before reacting. Take a breath and ask whether your reaction is based on fact or judgment. That gap gives you the power to choose.

**Focus on the present.** The past is gone and the future uncertain. The only moment in which you can act is now. Stoics encouraged doing the task at hand fully.

**Amor fati.** Love your fate. Accept what happens, even the hard parts, and look for ways to use them. Obstacles can become fuel for growth.

**Morning and evening routines.** In the morning, prepare for the day by anticipating challenges and setting intentions. In the evening, review what went well, what went wrong, and what you can improve. Salzgeber calls this a daily practice of self-examination and shows how Marcus Aurelius and Seneca used it.

**Voluntary discomfort.** Occasionally practice hardship on purpose, like taking cold showers, fasting, or going without luxuries. This builds resilience and shows you how little you really need.

**View from above.** Imagine looking down on your life from a great height, seeing your worries in proportion against the vastness of the world and time.

**Be a good person.** Act with kindness, honesty, and fairness, and ask what a wise person would do in your situation.

**Focus on your own actions.** Stop trying to control or change others. Work on your character, and let your example speak.

Other tools address handling insults, dealing with anger, avoiding comparison, accepting criticism, reducing desires, and building a life of simplicity.

## How the book teaches

Salzgeber's style is warm, direct, and free of jargon. He often shares how he applies the tools himself, including failures. Each tool ends with a short exercise or question, which helps convert the ideas into habit. He does not insist on perfection. In keeping with the Stoic tradition, he describes the philosopher as someone always making progress, never arriving.

He also notes that Stoicism is not about suppressing emotions. The Stoics wanted to avoid being controlled by destructive passions like rage and envy, but they valued healthy feelings such as joy and affection. The aim is to be unshaken, not unfeeling.

## Strengths and limits

The book's biggest advantage is its accessibility. It is short, well organized, and filled with quotes that readers can return to. Beginners get a solid foundation without being overwhelmed, and the tools make Stoicism feel like a set of daily practices rather than abstract theory.

On the other hand, readers who want deep philosophical analysis or historical context will find it light. It also offers a somewhat simplified, self-help version of Stoicism, and some of the more demanding aspects of the original, such as its views on fate and social duty, receive limited attention.

## What the reader takes away

The key lesson is that you have more power over your inner life than you think. By distinguishing what you can and cannot control, challenging your judgments, and practicing small habits of reflection, you can face difficulty with more calm and clarity.

A simple way to begin is to choose one or two tools, such as the morning preparation and evening review, and practice them for a few weeks. *The Little Book of Stoicism* is a friendly, practical companion for that journey, and an excellent first step into a philosophy that has helped people endure hardship for over two thousand years.
