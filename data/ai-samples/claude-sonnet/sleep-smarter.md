If you have ever dragged yourself through the day on too little sleep, you know how much it affects everything: mood, focus, appetite, patience, and energy. In *Sleep Smarter*, nutritionist and fitness expert Shawn Stevenson makes the case that sleep is the foundation of good health, and that most of us can improve it with a handful of practical changes. The book offers 21 strategies, organized around the idea that better nights lead to better days.

## What the book is about

Stevenson's own story gives the book its urgency. In his early twenties he was diagnosed with a degenerative disc condition and told he might have serious problems for life. Poor sleep, he came to believe, was a major contributor to his health struggles, and improving it became part of his recovery. From there he built a career helping others and eventually hosted a popular health podcast.

The central argument is that sleep is not wasted time. It is when the body repairs tissue, consolidates memory, balances hormones, and clears out waste from the brain. Chronic lack of sleep, by contrast, is linked to weight gain, weakened immunity, anxiety, depression, and a higher risk of serious disease. He suggests that we have normalized being tired, treating exhaustion as a sign of hard work, when in fact it undermines our performance.

## Why sleep matters

Early chapters explain the science in accessible terms. Stevenson describes the stages of sleep, including deep sleep and REM, and the role each plays. He explains the circadian rhythm, the body's internal clock, which responds strongly to light and darkness. When that rhythm is disrupted by late-night screens, irregular schedules, or too little daylight, sleep quality suffers.

He also discusses the connection between sleep and hormones. Poor sleep raises cortisol, the stress hormone, and disrupts the hormones that regulate hunger, which makes weight control harder. He argues that you cannot out-train or out-diet a sleep deficit.

## The strategies

The core of the book is a collection of tactics, which Stevenson groups around the day: what you do in the morning, afternoon, and evening, and how you set up your bedroom. A few of the main ones are below.

**Get morning sunlight.** Exposure to natural light early in the day helps anchor your circadian rhythm and boosts alertness. Spending some time outdoors, ideally within the first hours after waking, sends a clear signal to your body about when the day begins.

**Keep a consistent schedule.** Going to bed and waking up at roughly the same time, even on weekends, trains the body to expect sleep. Stevenson suggests aiming to be asleep by about 10 p.m., because the hours before midnight tend to be especially restorative.

**Watch caffeine.** Caffeine can linger in the body for many hours. He recommends cutting it off by early afternoon, or earlier if you are sensitive, and pays attention to hidden sources.

**Move your body.** Regular exercise improves sleep, but he cautions against intense workouts too close to bedtime for some people.

**Eat and drink smartly.** He suggests avoiding heavy meals and alcohol in the evening. Alcohol may make you sleepy but fragments your sleep later in the night. He also discusses foods and nutrients that can support rest, such as magnesium-rich options.

**Wind down.** Create an evening routine that signals your brain it is time to rest. This might include a warm bath, stretching, reading, or journaling to clear your mind of lingering worries.

**Limit blue light.** Phones, tablets, and televisions emit light that suppresses melatonin, the hormone that helps you fall asleep. Stevenson recommends turning screens off an hour or more before bed, using dimmer lighting in the evening, or wearing blue-light-blocking glasses if you cannot avoid screens.

## Setting up the bedroom

A significant portion of the book focuses on the sleep environment. He recommends making the room as dark as possible, using blackout curtains or a sleep mask, and keeping it cool, since a lower temperature supports better sleep. Noise should be minimized, and electronics should be moved out of the room or switched off. He also suggests removing the clutter and treating the bedroom as a place for sleep, not for work or scrolling.

He pays attention to the mattress and pillows as well, noting that comfort and support matter, and discusses reducing exposure to electromagnetic fields by keeping devices away from the bed. Some of these claims are less well established, and readers may decide for themselves how far to take them.

## Dealing with a busy mind

Stevenson recognizes that a racing mind is one of the biggest obstacles. He suggests writing down thoughts and tasks before bed, practicing gratitude, using deep breathing or meditation, and maintaining a relaxing pre-sleep ritual. Handling stress during the day, he notes, makes it easier to fall asleep at night.

## What the reader takes away

The lessons are straightforward: protect your sleep as a priority, keep a regular rhythm, manage light, and build a calm evening routine. Small changes tend to compound. You do not need to adopt all 21 strategies at once; choosing a few and sticking with them is more realistic.

A short list of starting points:

- Get outdoors in the morning.
- Stop caffeine early.
- Dim lights and shut off screens before bed.
- Keep your bedroom dark, cool, and quiet.
- Go to bed and wake up at consistent times.

The book is friendly, energetic, and easy to follow, though a few recommendations rely on thinner evidence than others. Overall, *Sleep Smarter* is an encouraging, practical guide for anyone who wants to wake up feeling more rested and live with more energy.
